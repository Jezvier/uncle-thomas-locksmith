Why a safesmith, not just a locksmith

Opening a safe is a different skill from opening a door. Safes are built to resist forced entry, and many have hidden relockers that fire if the safe is attacked the wrong way. A wrong approach can make the job far harder or damage what's inside. Few locksmiths in Singapore have formal safe training. Uncle Thomas spent a year in the United States training in safesmithing, and has since opened safes for multinational companies, on board ships and cruise liners, and in Thailand, Malaysia, China and Hong Kong.

Will my safe need to be drilled?

It depends on the safe and what's wrong with it. A safe with a forgotten code or a lost key can often be opened and stays fully usable. A safe whose lock has failed, such as a dead keypad board, a broken motor or a jammed bolt, usually needs drilling, because the lock can no longer respond to any code. Professional drilling is precise: a small hole at a specific point to reach the lock, chosen to avoid the relockers. On many safes the lock can then be replaced, and the safe used again. We'll always explain this, and the price, before starting. Read more in does my safe need drilling?

Often, yes. Safes with a forgotten code or lost key can usually be opened and stay usable. When the lock has failed, the safe usually needs precise drilling, and the lock can often be replaced afterwards. We explain the method and price before we start.
